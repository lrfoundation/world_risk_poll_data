::chapter The World Risk Poll across four editions | 4 | editions since 2019, the largest of them in **{v19:E02}** countries and territories

The World Risk Poll has now been run four times. Each edition has its own reports, and each report its own question. This chapter reads across them: what the Poll has asked since 2019, what has held, what has moved, and what four editions show together that no single one could.

Every figure in it is one that a report in this book has already published. Nothing has been recalculated for this chapter, and the notes at its end say where the reports disagree with one another.

# Four editions of one Poll

The first edition of the Poll went into the field in 2019, when some {v19:E01} people in {v19:E02} countries and territories were asked how safe they felt and what they thought threatened them. Its findings were published as a single volume. The Poll has been repeated every two years since, and each edition has kept a core of the original questions whilst adding new ones.

The second edition was shaped by the pandemic. Fieldwork in 2021 reached {cw:X01} people in {cw:X02} countries, {cw:X05} of which had also been surveyed in 2019. The third edition, in 2023, returned to {wa:X02} countries with nearly {wa:X01} interviews; the fourth, in 2025, covered {qh:X02} countries with more than {qh:X01}.

::box Reading across the editions

Three things complicate any comparison between editions, and they are worth holding in mind throughout this chapter.

**The countries differ.** The 2021 Poll covered fewer countries than the others, so a global figure for 2021 describes a slightly different world. Some reports handle this by comparing only the countries surveyed in both years, which is why the same year can carry two values. The share of people who felt less safe in 2019 is {v19:C1_1_less}% in the 2019 volume, on all {v19:E02} countries, and {cw:X06_less_2019}% in *A Changed World?*, on the {cw:X05} countries surveyed in both 2019 and 2021.

**The method differs.** Between 2021 and 2023 the survey moved from the telephone to face-to-face interviews in {wp:X20} countries, and *Engineering Safer Workplaces* shows that this matters: reported harm at work fell by an average of {wp:X21:abs} points where the method changed, against {wp:X23:abs} point where it did not.

**The questions differ.** Some were asked once; others were reworded. In 2023 people were asked whether they had experienced a disaster, in 2025 whether they had been impacted by one, and the two answers cannot be read as a trend.

Where two reports print different values for the same edition, this chapter uses the most recent report.

::endbox

# What has held

The first thing four editions show is how little the ranking of everyday risks moves. Asked in their own words to name the greatest risk to their safety in daily life, people have given the same answer most often in every edition: road-related accidents. The share was {qh:X29}% in 2019 (as a first answer, when two were asked for), {qh:X28}% in 2021, {qh:X27}% in 2023 and {qh:X08}% in 2025. Crime and violence ({qh:C1_3_global_2021}%, {qh:C1_3_global_2023}% and {qh:C1_3_global_2025}% in the last three editions) and personal health ({qh:X09}% in 2025) have followed, changing places only in 2025. Even in 2021, with interviews under way in the middle of a pandemic, Covid-19 was only the {cw:X08:rank} most commonly named risk ({cw:X07_covid}%).

Worry is steadier still. Around two in three people have been very or somewhat worried about harm from severe weather in every edition ({qh:C2_2_severe_weather_2019}% in 2019, {qh:C2_2_severe_weather_2025}% in 2025), and much the same is true of violent crime ({qh:C2_2_crime_2019}% and {qh:C2_2_crime_2025}%). Worry about food ({qh:C2_2_food_2019}% and {qh:C2_2_food_2025}%) and about drinking water ({qh:C2_2_water_2019}% and {qh:C2_2_water_2025}%) ended the period almost exactly where it began, although each dipped in 2021. Chart 1 sets the full series beside the share of people who were actually harmed.

Concern about climate change has been just as settled. The share who see it as a very serious threat to their country over the next 20 years has not moved by more than two points in six years: {at:C1_1_very_2019}% in 2019, {at:C1_1_very_2021}% in 2021, {at:C1_1_very_2023}% in 2023 and {at:X05}% in 2025. So, too, has the part of resilience that rests on other people. # What has moved

Steadiness is the larger part of the story, but it is not all of it. Five movements stand out across the editions, and they differ in kind: one was a spike, three look like trends, and one is a change in what people know.

## A loss of safety in 2021 that did not last

In 2019, {wa:C4_1_less_2019}% of people said they felt less safe than they had five years earlier. In 2021 the figure was {wa:C4_1_less_2021}%; by 2023 it was back to {wa:X10}%, and the share who felt more safe ({wa:X08}%) had recovered to its 2019 level ({wa:C4_1_more_2019}%). A single edition would have recorded either an ordinary year or an alarming one. Three editions show a shock and a recovery. The reports of the 2025 Poll do not return to the question.

## Rising worry about mental health

Mental health is the one risk asked about in every edition for which worry is clearly higher now than at the start: {qh:C2_2_mental_health_2019}% were very or somewhat worried in 2019, {qh:C2_2_mental_health_2021}% in 2021, {qh:C2_2_mental_health_2023}% in 2023 and {qh:C2_2_mental_health_2025}% in 2025. Importantly, the share who say they have personally been harmed has barely moved ({qh:C2_3_mental_health_2021}% in 2021, {qh:C2_3_mental_health_2025}% in 2025). Whether that reflects greater awareness, less stigma in naming the worry, or a real change in what people fear, the Poll alone cannot say.

## More harm from weather, food and water; less at work

Experience has shifted more than worry has. The share of adults personally harmed by severe weather in the previous two years rose from {qh:C2_3_severe_weather_2021}% in 2021 to {qh:C2_3_severe_weather_2023}% in 2023 and stood at {qh:C2_3_severe_weather_2025}% in 2025. Harm from food ({qh:C2_3_food_2021}%, then {qh:C2_3_food_2023}%, then {qh:X06}%) and from drinking water ({qh:C2_3_water_2021}%, {qh:C2_3_water_2023}% and {qh:X07}%) reached the highest levels the Poll has recorded.

Work moved the other way. Among the current workforce, {qh:X05}% had been seriously harmed at work in the two years before the 2021 Poll, {qh:X04}% before the 2023 Poll and {qh:X03}% before the 2025 Poll. It should be noted that part of the fall between 2021 and 2023 coincides with the change of survey method described above, so the 2025 figure is the firmer evidence of a decline.

# What each edition added

Not everything in the Poll is a trend. Each edition has also asked questions that were new, and some of the findings that have travelled furthest come from a single year.

**2019.** The first edition established how common serious injury at work is: {v19:X5_02}% of workers said they had been seriously injured at some point, and those in low-income countries ({v19:X5_12_low}%) were about twice as likely to say so as those in high-income countries ({v19:X5_12_high}%). It also recorded harm from the most basic of necessities, with {v19:X9_01}% saying they or someone they knew had been seriously harmed by food in the previous two years and {v19:X9_02}% by drinking water. On new technologies, genetically modified food was the one that more people expected to harm ({v19:X7_01}%) than to help ({v19:X7_02}%).

**2021.** The second edition measured the experience of violence and harassment at work in depth. {sw:X03}% of people who had ever worked had experienced it, and for most of them ({sw:X04}%) it had happened more than once. The same edition introduced the questions on resilience, and found household finances thin: {rw:X07}% could cover their basic needs for less than a month if they lost all income. On artificial intelligence, opinion had barely shifted since 2019 ({dw:X03}% expected it mostly to help, against {dw:X10_2019}% two years earlier), although {dw:C1_7_no}% said they would not feel safe in a car driven by a computer.

**2023.** The third edition turned to household waste, and found that {ww:X12}% of households dispose of it in an uncontrolled way; {ww:X13}% burn it. At work, it found that {wp:X07}% of the workforce had never received any safety and health training, rising to {wp:X10_never}% in agriculture, and that only {wp:X06}% of those who had been harmed told someone responsible for safety.

**2025.** The fourth edition widened the list of everyday hazards. Prolonged severe weather such as drought and heat had harmed {qh:X13}% of adults in two years, more than floods and storms had ({qh:X14}%), and {qh:X15}% had been harmed by the air they breathe even though {qh:X17}% were not worried about it at all. It also asked, for the first time, what people think others believe, and whether a warning could be acted on. Both are taken up below.

# What we have learned

What, then, do four editions show together? Five lessons recur across the reports in this book.

## The risks that harm most people are ordinary ones

When people are asked what threatens them, they do not name the events that fill the news. They name the road, their health and crime. When they are asked what has actually harmed them, the answers are the weather, work, traffic, food and water. No edition has disturbed that pattern; not even a pandemic displaced road accidents from the top of the list in 2021. The implication for anyone who works on safety is a plain one: the largest gains remain in the hazards of daily life.

## Worry is an unreliable guide to harm

The distance between what people fear and what happens to them is one of the oldest findings of the Poll and one of its most stable. In 2025, {qh:C2_2_traffic_2025}% of people were worried about traffic accidents and {qh:C2_3_traffic_2025}% had been harmed by one. Worry about severe weather was no higher in 2025 than in 2019, whilst harm from it rose between 2021 and 2025. Worry about mental health rose whilst harm barely did. And {qh:X17}% of adults are not worried at all about the air they breathe, although it had harmed {qh:X15}% of adults in the previous two years. Worry tells us what people will support and what they will overlook; it does not tell us where the harm is.

## Resilience is held up by communities, and is wearing thin in the individual

Three editions of the Resilience Index give the same reading. The total is stable, the community score has not moved, and the societal score has risen. The individual score has fallen in every edition. Whilst the fall is modest at each step, it is consistent, and it concerns the part of resilience that determines whether people believe they can do anything for themselves.

## A warning is only as good as the capacity to act on it

By 2025, {aa:X027}% of people who had been impacted by a disaster had received at least one warning. Reach, then, is no longer the main gap, although it remains uneven ({aa:X030_low}% in low-income countries against {aa:X030_high}% in high-income ones). The 2025 Poll asked the question that follows: could you act on the warning? {aa:X036}% of those warned said they could. Among people with both a household plan and a sense that they could protect themselves, the figure was {aa:X052_both}%; among those with neither, {aa:X052_neither}%. Read with the falling individual score above, this is perhaps the most practical finding of the four editions.

## The gap between richer and poorer countries appears in every edition

Each edition has found it in a different place. In 2019 it was injury at work, about twice as common among workers in low-income countries. In 2021 it was the sense of being able to protect one’s family from a disaster ({rw:X04_low_income}% in low-income countries, {rw:X04_high_income}% in high-income ones). In 2023 it was whether a household separates its waste ({ww:X08}% against {ww:X07}%). In 2025 it was whether a warning arrived. The subject changes with the questionnaire; the direction of the gap does not.

One further finding belongs here, although only one edition supports it so far. People underestimate one another. In 2025, {at:X05}% called climate change a very serious threat, but only {at:X13}% thought most other people in their country did. If that gap between private concern and perceived consensus holds in later editions, it will have been among the most useful things the Poll has measured.

## Closing remarks

A single survey can describe a moment. It takes several to tell a spike from a trend, and that is what the four editions now allow. The loss of safety recorded in 2021 turned out to be a spike. The decline in individual resilience, measured three times, looks like a trend. So does the rise in worry about mental health, although it eased a little in 2025. The fall in harm at work may be one; a fifth edition would say more.

The comparisons in this chapter are, however, only as good as the continuity of the questions behind them. Where the wording, the method or the list of countries changed, a trend was lost or blurred, and the reports themselves are candid about it. Whilst new questions are what keep the Poll useful, the value of the questions asked since 2019 grows with every edition that repeats them unchanged.

The reports that follow are printed in the order of the editions. Each can be read alone. Read in sequence, they show how little what people fear has changed in six years, and how much of what harms them still belongs to the ordinary business of daily life.
